    Practising SQL Server for the requests you actually get

    Most SQL courses teach syntax one keyword at a time. Work doesn’t arrive like that. It arrives as a message from someone who needs a number before a meeting: last week’s orders over £50, customers who haven’t bought anything, revenue by month for the finance pack. The hard part is rarely remembering what GROUP BY does. It’s turning a vague request into the right columns, the right rows and the right date range, then trusting the answer enough to send it. WorkSQL is practice for exactly that.

    What a ticket is

    A ticket is a short email from a colleague at Harbour & Co, a made-up company with the tables you’d find in most small businesses: Customers, Orders, OrderLines, Products, Returns and Staff. You read the request, open a query window, write T-SQL and run it against a real database. When you send the result, it’s checked against a known right answer. The marking is strict on purpose: SELECT *, an extra column, a wrongly named header or a missing ORDER BY all get sent back with a short note explaining why, the way a senior colleague would. Each ticket takes about five minutes, and there are 46 of them spread across seven simulated workdays.

    The problems that come up at the desk

    Dates. “Last week” and “the last 7 days” aren’t the same thing, and “not including today” trips people up constantly. You’ll practise building ranges with GETDATE, DATEADD and CAST(… AS date) that stop at midnight instead of leaking this morning’s orders into the report. The last week’s orders guide walks through the common versions.

    Joins. The two classic mistakes are losing rows and multiplying them. An inner join quietly drops customers who never ordered; a LEFT JOIN with IS NULL finds them. Joining Orders to OrderLines and then summing the order total counts each order once per line, which is why a JOIN can double your totals.

    Totals. Grouping is where SQL Server is strictest. Every column that isn’t inside an aggregate has to be in GROUP BY, or you get error 8120. Filtering on a total belongs in HAVING, not WHERE, which the WHERE vs HAVING guide explains.

    Reports. Later tickets cover the queries that end up on slides: CASE to label orders into bands, grouping by month, CTEs to keep long queries readable, and ROW_NUMBER to pick each customer’s latest order.

    Who it’s for

    Analysts and new starters who already use SQL Server Management Studio but feel slow with it. People moving from Excel, where a VLOOKUP becomes a JOIN and a pivot table becomes a GROUP BY: the Excel to SQL converters show the equivalents one function at a time. And anyone preparing for a data interview, where questions like top N per group, running totals and duplicates come up again and again: the interview practice page has those with a T-SQL editor.
